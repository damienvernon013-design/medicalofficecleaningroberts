EPA List N Disinfectants and Correct Dwell Times

What practice managers in Wisconsin need to know about clinical disinfection products

What is EPA List N?

EPA List N is the Environmental Protection Agency's registry of disinfectants effective against SARS-CoV-2 and other enveloped viruses. For clinical facility managers, the significance isn't the COVID-19 connection — it's that EPA List N products meet the agency's efficacy standards for use in healthcare settings and are registered under FIFRA (the Federal Insecticide, Fungicide, and Rodenticide Act).

Using a registered disinfectant matters because it creates a defensible record. If your practice faces an infection control inquiry, you can point to the EPA registration number of the product your cleaner used, verify its approved uses, and confirm that the application was compliant. An unregistered or general-purpose cleaner doesn't give you that.

Dwell time — the most commonly violated requirement

A disinfectant's dwell time (also called contact time) is the amount of time the product must remain visibly wet on a surface to achieve the claimed kill rate against pathogens. This isn't a guideline — it's a condition of the EPA registration. A product that claims a 2-log reduction in a 30-second contact time only achieves that reduction if the surface stays wet for 30 seconds.

In practice, many cleaning crews apply a disinfectant spray and immediately wipe it. On a dry exam room counter, a spray may evaporate in 10–15 seconds. If the product's required contact time is 2 minutes, no meaningful disinfection has occurred. The surface looks clean. It isn't.

Common dwell time ranges for clinical-grade disinfectants:

Product Type Typical Dwell Time Notes
Quaternary ammonium (quats) 30 seconds – 10 minutes Varies widely by formulation and pathogen target
Sodium hypochlorite (bleach-based) 1 – 10 minutes Effective against C. diff spores at higher concentrations
Hydrogen peroxide 30 seconds – 5 minutes Accelerated formulas achieve shorter contact times
Alcohol-based 30 seconds – 2 minutes Limited residual effect; not suitable for all surface types

The specific dwell time for any product is on the product label, and the label is the law under FIFRA. A cleaning contractor should be able to tell you exactly which product they use in your exam rooms and what its required contact time is.

Surface compatibility

Not every disinfectant is appropriate for every surface. Bleach-based products can corrode metal, damage vinyl, and degrade certain plastics over time. Alcohol-based products may damage rubber gaskets. The EPA registration specifies approved surface types, and the product label includes material compatibility notes. A cleaning crew using a high-concentration bleach solution on dental chair vinyl is causing equipment damage in the name of disinfection.

Our written scope documents which product is used on which surface type in your facility. That documentation exists partly to protect your equipment, not just to satisfy compliance.

Verifying your cleaner's products

Any cleaning contractor working in a clinical facility should be able to provide the EPA registration numbers of the disinfectants they use and confirm the required dwell times. If they can't, they aren't following the registration requirements — which means their disinfection isn't achieving what the product claims.
